## Genetic Algorithm for Phototaxis

This activity explores evolutionary robotics through a genetic algorithm that optimizes the weights of a neural network controller for a foot-bot performing phototaxis. In the provided setup, the genome is a vector of 50 real values, each representing a neural-network weight, and the GA evaluates each candidate by writing the genome into an environment variable and launching ARGoS to simulate the robot behavior. The controller receives the 24 light sensor readings as inputs and maps them directly to the two wheel speeds, so evolution does not design the behavior explicitly but searches for a weight configuration that produces effective movement toward the light.

The code follows the standard GA cycle: initialization, evaluation, selection, crossover, mutation, and replacement. The population is first created randomly with genes sampled in [-1,1], then each individual is evaluated several times through the `fitness()` function, which runs ARGoS and reads the fitness printed on stdout. In the tested version, mutation is implemented by adding Gaussian noise with mean 0 and standard deviation 1 to each gene with probability `MUTATION_RATE`.

`N_EVAL` controls how many times the same genome is tested before assigning its fitness. Its role is to reduce the effect of stochasticity in simulation: with multiple evaluations, the assigned score is more reliable because it aggregates repeated trials rather than trusting a single run. If `N_EVAL` is set to 1, the algorithm becomes faster, but selection becomes noisier because an individual may be rewarded or penalized due to one lucky or unlucky simulation. When `N_EVAL = 3` and the minimum is used instead of the mean, the fitness becomes a worst-case estimate rather than an average-case estimate, so evolution favors robust controllers that never fail badly, even if they are less impressive on average.

For Task 7, the comparison among crossover rates shows that `CX_RATE = 0` produced the best mean fitness over five replicas, with mean 0.874410 and median 0.849600, while `CX_RATE = 1` reached mean 0.821496 and median 0.816925, and `CX_RATE = 0.5` was the weakest with mean 0.746126 and median 0.800972. In this configuration, mutation alone appears sufficient to explore the search space, while crossover did not provide a clear benefit and in the intermediate setting even reduced performance. This suggests that, for this simple genome and short evolutionary run, recombination may disrupt good weight combinations more often than it helps assemble better ones.

For Task 8, elitism was implemented by sorting the population by fitness, copying the best `ELITE_SIZE` individuals directly into the next generation, and filling the remaining slots with offspring. For Task 9, the comparison between full replacement and elitism shows that elitism improved the average outcome: replacement obtained mean 0.704019 and median 0.681010, while elitism with `ELITE_SIZE = 5` obtained mean 0.775891 and median 0.721182. This is consistent with the purpose of elitism, which protects good solutions from being lost by unlucky variation and helps preserve progress across generations.

For Task 10, tournament selection was implemented in its simplest form by sampling two individuals at random and returning the one with higher fitness. For Task 11, elitism was kept and the tournament selection outperformed roulette-wheel selection in the reported runs: proportional selection achieved mean 0.776458 and median 0.699608, whereas tournament selection achieved mean 0.903179 and median 0.942462. The result indicates that tournament selection provided stronger and more stable selective pressure in this experiment, while roulette-wheel selection was more sensitive to noisy fitness differences and therefore less consistent.

Each run was summarized by the fitness of its best individual, rather than by an average over the whole run; this choice influences the final comparison, and alternative summaries such as the mean or the minimum fitness could also be tested. 

This laboratory highlights how the tested parameters balance the two main goals of Evolutionary Computation: exploring the search space to discover new solutions (like mutation and crossover), and exploiting the fittest individuals to converge toward a local optimum (like elitism and tournament selection).
