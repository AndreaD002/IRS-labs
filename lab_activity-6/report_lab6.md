## Swarm Robotics: Aggregation Task

This laboratory explores swarm robotics through an aggregation task, where a decentralized group of robots must gather in a single location using local interactions. The behavior is modeled as a probabilistic automaton based on the observation of social insects. Each robot continuously updates its probability to stop or to resume walking based on the number of nearby stopped robots, which it detects through its range-and-bearing (RAB) communication system. 

The core logic relies on two opposing feedback loops. A positive feedback loop encourages stopping: the more stopped robots a wandering robot detects, the higher its probability to stop ($P_s$). A negative feedback loop encourages walking: a robot at the boundary of a group detects fewer stopped neighbors than one inside the group, raising its probability of leaving ($P_w$). Over time, this dynamic breaks up small unstable clusters and consolidates the swarm into larger, stable aggregates.

The provided implementation uses specific parameter choices to drive this dynamic effectively. The spontaneous walking probability is set unusually high ($W = 0.85$), meaning that in the absence of a large cluster, a stopped robot is very likely to resume moving. This high baseline acts as a strong dispersive force that drifts small clusters apart, preventing premature convergence on minor groups. To counteract this high $W$ once a cluster *does* form, the $\alpha$ weight is also set high ($\alpha = 0.85$). This means $P_s$ scales aggressively with the number of neighbors, ensuring that once a group reaches a critical mass, passing robots almost certainly stop and join it. In essence, the two rules operate somewhat asymmetrically: stopping is heavily driven by local density (high $\alpha$), while walking away has a high independent baseline ($W$) that is only suppressed by a dense neighborhood. 

When wandering, the robot executes a random walk combined with obstacle avoidance. Proximity sensors on the front-left and front-right are evaluated, and if an object is closer than `PROX_THRESHOLD`, the robot pivots away by reversing the wheel furthest to the obstacle (like a Breitenberg achitecture). Because the obstacle avoidance behavior inherently repels robots from one another and fights the formation of tight clusters, the communication range was increased to `MAXRANGE = 35` cm. This allows the robots to sense the aggregate and update their probabilities from slightly further away, bridging the gap created by their physical avoidance radius.

In the black-spot version (file "aggregation_on_black.lua"), once the robot reaches the black area, two values are used to increase the stopping probability and decrease the wandering probability. At the same time, the influence of nearby robots was weakened to avoid forming stable clusters outside the target area. As a result, robots can leave small groups more easily, while clusters formed on the black spot remain much more stable because stopping there is favored and leaving is harder.

In conclusion, the high sensitivity of the swarm to these parameters highlights the limitations of manual tuning; automating this design process with a Genetic Algorithm (GA) would likely yield more robust and optimal behaviors.
